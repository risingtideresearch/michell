michell · PR #29 · exact control gradients

Nine handles, one pass

The michell solver already told you how much wave drag a hull has. PR #29 makes it tell you something better: for every shape control at once, exactly, from a single solve — which way to move it to make the drag go down. This page rebuilds the whole idea from a picture of a hull, one small step at a time. Every figure below runs the same mathematics as the Rust code, live, and is checked against the solver's own output.

01A hull with nine handles

Here is the hull this PR ships its tests on: the Wigley hull, a classic benchmark shape — 10 m long, 1 m beam, 0.625 m draft. The solver stores it as a smooth surface controlled by nine numbers arranged in a 3×3 grid: three positions along the length (bow, midship, stern) × three depths (waterline, mid-depth, keel). Each number is a control ordinate in meters — a knob that pulls the smooth surface outward from the centerline near its region. The surface does not pass through the control values themselves: the classic Wigley's midship-waterline control is 1.0 m, but the surface's actual half-breadth there is 0.5 m, because the B-spline blends neighboring controls.

Think of the nine numbers as handles. Drag one below and watch the hull's width change nearby — and only nearby.

Two questions matter for design:

1. How much wave drag does this shape have? The solver answered that before this PR.

2. Which handles should move, and which way, to reduce it? That is the derivative of drag with respect to each handle — nine numbers. Before this PR, getting them meant running the whole solver eighteen times. After it, they come out of one solve, exactly. That trick, and why you can trust it, is the story of this page.

02The drag you can see from the shore

A moving ship leaves a wake — the familiar V-shaped pattern of waves spreading out behind it. Those waves carry energy away, and that energy has to come from somewhere: the engine. The drag force the ship feels from making waves is wave resistance, and for slender fast ships it is a large share of the total.

So question 1 becomes: given the hull shape, how big are the waves it makes? Answer that and you can add up the energy they carry. The rest of the first half of this page builds that answer.

A note on the speed axis: naval architects measure speed with the Froude number Fn = U/√(g L) — speed scaled by boat length. Fn 0.35 for our 10 m hull is about 3.5 m/s (6.7 knots). The same Fn means the same wave pattern at any scale.

03Michell's move: only the slope matters

In 1898 J. H. Michell noticed something that still powers this solver: if the hull is thin — long compared to its beam — then what makes waves is not the hull's width itself but how fast the width changes as you move from bow to stern. Water gets pushed apart where the hull widens and closes back in where it narrows. The hull acts like a sheet of little sources (bow half) and sinks (stern half) whose strength is the longitudinal slope ∂f/∂x.

Two symbols, both of which you have already met as pictures:

f(x, z)
the half-breadth surface from §1 — hull width at position x along the length, depth z below the waterline. The nine handles define it.
∂f/∂x
its slope along the length — the source strength above. This is the only way the hull shape enters the whole calculation.

Keep that last sentence; it is load-bearing later. One consequence you can check right now: making the whole hull uniformly wider (adding the same amount to all nine handles) does not change ∂f/∂x anywhere — and indeed the solver returns exactly the same wave resistance for it. Only change in width makes waves. (This move is available because the nine-handle design space doesn't force the ends closed; a closed hull couldn't widen uniformly without opening its bow and stern.)

04One wave direction at a time

The wake in §2 looks complicated, but it is a sum of simple ingredients: flat plane waves, each traveling at some angle θ off the ship's track. Deep-water physics pins down everything about the wave once you pick its angle: a wave riding steadily with the ship must match the ship's speed along its own direction, and that fixes its wavelength. Straight-behind waves (θ = 0) are the longest; waves angled outward are shorter.

The solver labels each direction not by θ but by

λ = sec θ = 1/cos θ
λ
the direction label. λ = 1 is straight behind; λ → ∞ approaches sideways. Nothing deep — just a change of variable that makes the formulas cleaner.

Plan for the next two sections: for each direction λ, ask how much of that wave does the hull make? (§5), then add up the energy over all directions (§6).

05Adding wavelets: the amplitude F(λ)

Fix one direction λ. Every little patch of hull surface, with its source strength ∂f/∂x, emits a wavelet in that direction. The wavelets don't simply add: each arrives with a phase — a timing offset set by where along the hull it was born. Bow wavelets and stern wavelets can reinforce or cancel.

The natural bookkeeping for "amplitude plus timing" is an arrow in the plane (a complex number): length = strength, angle = phase. To total up the hull's output, walk along the hull adding each patch's little arrow tip-to-tail. Watch it happen:

The end-to-end arrow is the complex wave amplitude:

F(λ) = I(λ) + i J(λ) = ∬ ∂f∂x e−νλ²z eiνλx dx dz
F(λ)
the total arrow you just watched being drawn — one complex number per direction.
I, J
its two components. Two numbers because a wave needs an amplitude and a phase.
ν
"nu" = g/U², a wavenumber set by speed alone. It is the only place speed enters. (For Fn 0.35: ν ≈ 0.816 m⁻¹.)
e−νλ²z
the depth fade — deep hull barely makes surface waves.
eiνλx
the phase turn as the marcher moves along the hull — what makes the trace curl.

The integral sign is doing exactly what the animation does: summing arrows over the hull surface. Nothing more.

06From amplitude to drag

Energy in a wave goes as amplitude squared. So direction λ contributes |F(λ)|² = I² + J², and the total wave resistance adds that over all directions with a known geometric weight:

R = C ∫1∞ |F(λ)|² λ²√(λ²−1) dλ, C = 4ρg²πU²
R
wave resistance, in newtons. The answer to question 1.
|F(λ)|²
the squared length of §5's arrow — energy per direction.
λ²/√(λ²−1)
a fixed geometric weight over the wave fan; not shape-dependent.
C
physics prefactor: water density ρ, gravity g, speed U. Constant for a given run.

This is Michell's 1898 integral, exactly as the solver's documentation states it. Here it is for our hull — the energy spectrum across the fan, and the resulting drag curve over speed:

Where we are. Hull shape → source strength ∂f/∂x → one complex arrow F(λ) per wave direction → energy |F|² → weighted sum over directions = drag R. That is the entire forward calculation. Still missing: how the code evaluates it fast (§7), and the real prize — the derivative of R with respect to each of the nine handles (§8–§11).

07Computing R: exact inside, careful outside

The surface f is built from polynomial pieces (that's what a B-spline is). And the inner integral — the arrow-summing of §5 — is a polynomial times exponentials, which has a closed form. So the solver evaluates F(λ) by formula: no quadrature error, no geometry discretization — the only error left in the inner integral is ordinary floating-point roundoff in evaluating that formula. The only numerical approximation in the whole solver is the outer sum over directions.

That outer integral gets awkward near λ = 1 (the √(λ²−1) below the line blows up), so the code substitutes λ = sec θ — the same θ from §4 — which soaks up the singularity and leaves a smooth integrand on θ from 0 to 90°:

R = C ∫0π/2 |F(sec θ)|² sec³θ dθ

The integrand oscillates faster and faster as θ grows (short waves rack up phase quickly), so the code slices θ into panels sized to the local oscillation rate — wide panels where the integrand is lazy, narrow where it wiggles — and integrates each panel with a fixed 16-point Gauss–Legendre rule. Then it halves all the panels and does it again, until two passes agree to tolerance:

The first change in this PR lives right here, and it is deliberately boring. Each panel needs sin θ, cos θ, sec θ, tan θ; the old code computed them separately, and regenerated the 16 Gauss–Legendre nodes and weights on every refinement pass. The new code computes sine and cosine together, derives the rest (tan θ = sin θ · sec θ), and creates the 16-point rule once per solve. Algebraically identical — but not bitwise: a last-ulp difference in tan θ can shift panel edges slightly. Measured effect in the low-Froude benchmark: 235,584 → 235,808 inner evaluations, resistance checksums agreeing to all 12 printed digits. Same mathematics, same answer, a bit less arithmetic (the 21-speed sweep ran 7–11% faster).

08Now do it eighteen times

On to question 2. The classical way to get the derivative of R with respect to handle Pk is centered finite differences: nudge the handle out by a small h, solve; nudge it in, solve; subtract:

∂R∂Pk ≈ R(Pk+h) − R(Pk−h)2h

Two full solves per handle. Nine handles: eighteen solves for one gradient. A real design surface has far more handles than nine, and an optimizer wants a fresh gradient at every step. Watch the cost pile up — these are the real evaluation counts from the solver at Fn 0.35:

Finite differences have a second problem besides cost: you must pick h. Too large and the answer is polluted by curvature; too small and you subtract two nearly equal numbers and the digits cancel. We will see that dilemma measured, in §13 — where it becomes a virtue, as an independent check on the exact gradient.

09The load-bearing fact: F is linear in the handles

Look back at the chain: handles → f → ∂f/∂x → F(λ). Every arrow in that chain is plain scaling-and-adding. The surface f is a weighted sum of the handles (that is what "B-spline" means). Taking ∂/∂x keeps it a weighted sum. Integrating wavelets keeps it a weighted sum. So for each direction λ,

F(λ) = Σk Pk Bk(λ)
Pk
handle k's value — one of the nine numbers.
Bk(λ)
a fixed basis arrow: the wave amplitude the hull would have if handle k were 1 and all others 0. Depends on geometry and speed, not on the handle values.

So the hull's total arrow is just nine fixed arrows, each scaled by its handle, laid tip-to-tail. Drag the slider: one arrow stretches, the rest hold still, and F slides along a straight line.

Linear things have easy derivatives: ∂F/∂Pk is simply the fixed arrow Bk(λ). No approximation, no step size h. If the whole pipeline were linear we would be done — but there is exactly one nonlinear step left.

10The only nonlinear step is the square

Energy is |F|², and squaring is not linear. But its derivative is the one you learned first: y = x² gives dy = 2x·dx. The complex version has one twist worth seeing rather than reading. Nudge F by a small arrow ε·B. Only the part of the nudge along F changes F's length; the crosswise part just rotates it. So:

∂|F|²/∂Pk = 2 Re( F · Bk )
F · Bk
complex product of F's conjugate with the basis arrow; its real part is exactly "the component of Bk along F, times |F|" — the dot product of the two arrows.
2
the same 2 as in 2x·dx.

Put §9 and §10 together and the derivative of the whole pipeline is in hand, in closed form:

∂R∂Pk = C ∫ 2 Re( F(λ) · Bk(λ) ) w(λ) dλ

— the same outer integral as R itself, with |F|² replaced by 2Re(F̄·Bk). No step size h anywhere. This is what "exact gradient" means in the PR title: the analytic derivative of the very number the solver returns.

11One pass backward

Computing that integral naively for each k would still mean nine more outer integrals. The implementation is smarter, and if you have met neural-network backpropagation this will feel familiar. Run the normal resistance solve once — adaptive refinement and all. Then revisit only the final pass's quadrature nodes one more time. At each node the amplitude F is already determined, so the factor 2Re(F̄ · —) is a fixed linear functional: evaluate it against every local polynomial coefficient at once, accumulate with the same panel weights, and finally push the result backward through the (linear!) map from handles to polynomial coefficients — its transpose, applied once.

Three things make this trustworthy rather than merely clever:

— The reverse pass uses the same nodes, weights, panel sizes, truncation point, and prefactor as the resistance it differentiates. It is the derivative of the discrete number the solver actually returns, not of an idealized cousin. Debug assertions even require the reverse pass to reproduce the primal resistance on its way through.

— The transpose step is not a new approximation. The forward map from handles to span coefficients is linear; running a linear map backward is exact by construction.

— The number of solves no longer grows with the handle count: one solve plus one reverse sweep, versus 2N solves for finite differences. (The sweep and the control-map transpose still loop over the spline's spans and basis functions, so their cost grows mildly with a finer control net — the PR is explicit about this.) 22,896 + 15,392 = 38,288 evaluations against 412,128 for finite differences — 10.8× fewer here, and the ratio grows with every handle a finer design surface adds.

Where we are. Resistance is a weighted sum of |F(λ)|²; F is linear in the handles; the square's derivative is 2Re(F̄·B); so one backward sweep of the final quadrature delivers all nine ∂R/∂Pk exactly. Left to do: watch it work (§12), audit the evidence that the code implements it correctly (§13), time it (§14), and draw its boundaries honestly (§15).

13How we know it's right

"The derivation looks correct" is not evidence. The PR's case rests on independent checks with different failure modes — if the code were wrong, several unrelated alarms would have to fail together to hide it.

Finite differences as referee. The committed test compares every analytic derivative against centered finite differences at h = 10⁻⁵ and requires agreement within 2×10⁻⁶ — through perturbed full solves that never touch the reverse-pass code. And the step-size sweep shows the textbook finite-difference signature, which is itself diagnostic:

An oracle that shares no code. An independent Wigley implementation — analytic basis integrals, dense Simpson outer rule, none of the production span/moment/adjoint code — agreed with the resistance to 1.8×10⁻⁷ and with all nine derivatives to 3.1×10⁻⁷. A second disposable surface with a corner in it (a repeated C⁰ knot, which the smooth Wigley never exercises) passed all 15 of its derivative checks too.

Invariants. Uniform widening makes no waves (§3), so the nine derivatives must sum to zero. The test demands <10⁻¹¹ relative; this page's run of the solver measured the sum at 7×10⁻¹⁵ against gradient magnitudes of order 10². A broken control-map transpose or basis normalization would break this instantly.

Sabotage. Mutation testing: deliberately break the implementation in realistic ways and confirm the tests catch it.

(This page adds one more informal check: its JavaScript reimplementation reproduces the solver's R and all nine gradients to the precision shown — see the console.)

14How much faster

The deeper point is not 16× — it is the scaling. Finite differences cost 2N solves for N handles; the reverse pass costs one solve plus one sweep whose own cost grows only mildly with N (it walks the spline's spans and basis functions, not the handles one at a time). At nine handles the ratio is 14–16×; a fifty-handle design surface would push it toward two orders of magnitude.

15What “exact” does not mean

“Exact” here has a precise, narrow meaning: the analytic derivative of the discrete number the solver returns. Worth stating what that does not cover — the PR itself is explicit about all of these:

— Michell's thin-ship model is an approximation of physics. The gradient is exact for the model, not for the ocean.

— The returned gradient inherits the resistance's quadrature discretization and tail truncation. In one adversarial low-Froude case the outer integrator stopped at its evaluation cap and the reported est_rel_error (~10⁻¹⁰) badly understated a true 2.8% error — a pre-existing limitation this PR neither introduced nor fixed, but which its audit quantified. Treat est_rel_error as a refinement diagnostic, not a delivered-accuracy guarantee.

— The derivative moves handle values only. Knots, spline degrees, speed, gravity, and fluid stay fixed.

— Symmetric hulls only, for now: an asymmetric hull has two control nets, and the API rejects it rather than return an ambiguous half-answer.

— A proposed handle update must still satisfy geometry (control values can't go negative, and a real hull needs closed ends — neither is enforced by the gradient). The gradient tells you the direction; constraints stay your job.
