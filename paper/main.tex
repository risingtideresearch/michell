\begin{abstract}
\itshape
At low Froude number, Michell's outer integral grows oscillatory and
complicates algebraic-tail estimation.  Michelsen's 1960 dissertation and
1972 sequel reduced it for polynomial and orthogonal-basis hulls.  For
tensor-product B-spline hulls of degree at most 16 per direction,
repeated integration by parts gives exact endpoint waves.  Pair expansion
gives analytically continued Bickley kernels
\(
K_s(\omega)=\int_1^\infty
\e^{\iu\omega\lambda}\lambda^{-s}(\lambda^2-1)^{-1/2}\,d\lambda,
\).
An absolute bound controls submerged terms; contour rotation makes work
frequency-independent.  The method accepts results only when the total error
estimate meets the requested tolerance; otherwise it
reverts to direct real-axis integration.  For the standard Wigley hull at
\(\Fn=0.02\), it uses 1,152 kernel nodes rather than 511,504 inner-transform
evaluations, reduces median runtime from
\SI{21.541}{ms} to \SI{0.030}{ms} (about 700-fold), and differs from an
independent real-axis reference by \(1.83\times10^{-13}\) relatively.  The
reduction applies only to upright symmetric monohulls with coarse,
well-separated endpoints; the contour estimate is empirical, not a
formal interval certificate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In 1898, \citet{Michell1898} expressed the wave resistance of a thin ship as a
one-dimensional integral of the squared Fourier--Laplace transform of its
longitudinal slope.  \citet{Tuck1989} later placed that formula in the wider
practice of thin-ship calculation.  Michell's formula remains inexpensive
beside nonlinear free-surface methods, yet retains the bow--stern interference
that couples resistance to speed and hull shape.  \citet{Lazauskas2009} uses
it for preliminary design and multihull studies, while
\citet{DambrinePierreRousseaux2016} uses it for mathematical shape
optimization.  At low speed, however, a fast result is useful only when its
error estimate can be trusted.

The analytic response to this numerical problem has a long history.
\citet{Michelsen1960}, building on Birkhoff and Kotik's transformation,
separated the calculation into a hull function and a speed-dependent Michell
function.  He derived a convergent special-function series for polynomial
hull functions and proposed tabulation for systematic design.
\citet{Michelsen1963} next treated polynomial centerline singularity
distributions, and \citet{Michelsen1966} developed high- and low-speed
asymptotic approximations.  \citet{Michelsen1972} then used Gegenbauer source
distributions and orthogonality to reduce the integral to a finite double sum.
The verified abstract of \citet{SendagortaGrases1988} describes the same broad
program: rapidly convergent series, products of shape functions, and
shape-independent velocity functions for tabulation and computer-aided
design.  The present B-spline realization extends this lineage with measured
error accounting and a selection rule that can revert to direct integration.

Its numerical difficulty is equally old.  Let \(\nu=g/U^2\), where \(U\) is
speed and \(g\) gravitational acceleration.  As \(U\), and therefore
\(\Fn=U/\sqrt{gL}\), decrease, the phase \(\nu\lambda x\) oscillates ever more
rapidly over the unbounded coordinate \(\lambda\).  \citet{Tuck1989} used
Filon-like longitudinal integration.  \citet{Lazauskas2009} used exact
piecewise-quadratic formulas with a fixed angular rule and reported that very
low Froude number remains exceptional.  Direct adaptive integration of the
outer integral must do more work as frequency rises, then decide where to
truncate an oscillatory algebraic tail.

The error estimate matters as much as the work.  At the pre-fix revision
\texttt{64925d4}, one quiet-window criterion reported
\(5.123\times10^{-9}\) relative error at \(\Fn=0.05\); the measured error was
\(5.781\times10^{-7}\), about 113 times larger.  The frozen kernel advances
quiet windows by oscillatory phase alone and adds refinement and tail
estimates.  All four low-Froude calculations reported below stop at the
refinement cap.  They are measured comparators, not claims that the requested
\(10^{-8}\) tolerance was achieved.

The endpoint and contour ingredients also come from earlier work.
\citet{Wehausen1973} surveys classical thin-ship theory and its limits.
\citet{KellerAhluwalia1976} shows that bow and stern waterline data control the
small-Froude wave field and resistance.  \citet[pp.~83--85]{Gotman2002} gives
finite endpoint-derivative sums and products that separate bow, stern, and
bow--stern interactions.  \citet{HuybrechsVandewalle2006} develops numerical
steepest descent, and \citet{Motygin2017} applies it to the Kelvin
Green-function integral in linear ship-wave theory.

We use these ingredients to decompose every polynomial B-spline span into
finite endpoint terms, including interior and full-multiplicity chine knots,
and to bound every resistance pair containing an omitted submerged endpoint.
We then evaluate the retained, analytically continued Bickley kernels on a
frequency-scaled Gaussian contour.  The method accepts the reduced result only
when its error estimate meets the requested tolerance; otherwise it reverts to
direct real-axis integration.  This is the meaning of ``error-gated'' in the
title.

These changes implement established analytic and numerical ideas; they do not
claim method priority.  The accessible publisher and index records establish
only the statements made here for \citet{Michelsen1972} and
\citet{SendagortaGrases1988}.  Their full texts remain due-diligence items.

\section{Michell resistance for B-spline hulls}
\label{sec:michell}

Michell's formula maps the longitudinal hull slope to a free-wave spectrum.
Its squared magnitude determines the resistance.  Let \(y=f(x,z)\geq0\) be the
half-breadth of an upright symmetric hull, with \(z\geq0\) measured downward
from the undisturbed waterline.  We use the normalization
\begin{align}
  \Rw &=\frac{4\rho g^2}{\pi U^2}
  \int_1^\infty \frac{\lambda^2}{\sqrt{\lambda^2-1}}
  \left|F(\lambda)\right|^2\,d\lambda,
  \label{eq:michell}\\
  F(\lambda) &= \iint_\Omega f_x(x,z)
  \e^{-\nu\lambda^2 z}\e^{\iu\nu\lambda x}\,dx\,dz,
  \qquad \nu=\frac{g}{U^2}.
  \label{eq:inner}
\end{align}
We take \(g=\SI{9.80665}{m.s^{-2}}\), freshwater density
\(\rho=\SI{999.1}{kg.m^{-3}}\), and the length Froude number
\(\Fn=U/\sqrt{gL}\).  We report
\begin{equation}
 C_w=\frac{\Rw}{\tfrac12\rho U^2 S_w},\qquad
 S_w=2\iint_\Omega\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_z^2}\,dx\,dz,
 \label{eq:cw}
\end{equation}
where \(S_w\) is the two-sided wetted area of the B-spline hull.  Lengths,
speeds, and forces are in metres, metres per second, and newtons; \(\lambda\),
\(\Fn\), and \(C_w\) are dimensionless.

To evaluate the inner transform without station or waterline sampling, we
represent the hull as a clamped, non-rational tensor-product B-spline surface.
On each knot rectangle \(S=[x_0,x_1]\times[z_0,z_1]\), its longitudinal
derivative is an exact bivariate polynomial,
\begin{equation}
  f_x|_S=\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\sum_{b=0}^{q}
  c^{S}_{ab}(x-x_0)^a(z-z_0)^b.
  \label{eq:spanpoly}
\end{equation}
We obtain these coefficients once from exact spline derivatives at the span
corners.  Repeated knots create zero-length intervals, which we discard.  An
interior knot whose multiplicity equals the degree represents a continuous
chine exactly.

\section{The endpoint reduction}
\label{sec:endpoint}

The spline representation concentrates the inner transform into waves attached
to span endpoints.  Finite polynomial degree makes this reduction exact:
repeated integration by parts terminates.  For a polynomial \(p\) of degree
\(m\),
\begin{align}
 \int_{x_a}^{x_b} p(x)\e^{\iu kx}\,dx
 &=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^r}{(\iu k)^{r+1}}
   \left[p^{(r)}(x)\e^{\iu kx}\right]_{x_a}^{x_b},
 \label{eq:xparts}\\
 \int_{z_a}^{z_b} p(z)\e^{-\kappa z}\,dz
 &=-\sum_{u=0}^{m}\frac{1}{\kappa^{u+1}}
   \left[p^{(u)}(z)\e^{-\kappa z}\right]_{z_a}^{z_b}.
 \label{eq:zparts}
\end{align}
We apply \cref{eq:xparts,eq:zparts} to every monomial in
\cref{eq:spanpoly}, taking \(k=\nu\lambda\) and
\(\kappa=\nu\lambda^2\).  This gives an exact endpoint representation.

\begin{proposition}[Finite endpoint representation]
For a polynomial tensor-product B-spline hull with longitudinal degree at least
one, the transform in \cref{eq:inner} can be written
\begin{equation}
 F(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{M} c_j\lambda^{-n_j}
 \e^{\iu\nu\lambda x_j}\e^{-\nu\lambda^2z_j},
 \qquad n_j\geq3,
 \label{eq:endpointsum}
\end{equation}
where \((x_j,z_j)\) are endpoints of nonzero knot spans.  The complex
coefficients \(c_j\) are finite combinations of span-polynomial endpoint
derivatives, and equal endpoint/power triples combine exactly.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Applying \cref{eq:xparts,eq:zparts} replaces a monomial of derivative orders
\((r,u)\) with endpoint factors proportional to
\[
(\nu\lambda)^{-(r+1)}(\nu\lambda^2)^{-(u+1)}.
\]
The resulting power of \(\lambda\) is \(n=r+2u+3\).  Both sums terminate at
the polynomial degrees.  Summing the finite contributions from every nonzero
knot rectangle gives \cref{eq:endpointsum}.
\end{proof}

We compare \cref{eq:endpointsum} directly with independent exact-moment
calculations for the Wigley hull and for a multi-span hull with a
full-multiplicity chine.  Across \(1\leq\lambda\leq20\), every discrepancy is
below the scaled \(5\times10^{-10}\) threshold.

\section{The error bound and method selection}
\label{sec:bound}

At low speed, every wave source below the waterline is exponentially
suppressed.  The decomposition makes that suppression visible and bounds the
cost of discarding those sources.  We partition \cref{eq:endpointsum} into
waterline terms \(W\), for which \(z_j=0\), and submerged terms \(S\), for
which \(z_j>0\).  Because \(\lambda\geq1\), every submerged term contains at
least \(\e^{-\nu z_j}\).  Retaining only \(W\) gives
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde F(\lambda)=\sum_{j\in W}c_j\lambda^{-n_j}
 \e^{\iu\nu\lambda x_j}.
 \label{eq:waterline}
\end{equation}

Before outer integration, we expand \(|\widetilde F|^2\).  This removes the
nonanalytic complex conjugation from the integrand:
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde R_{\mathrm w}=C
 \sum_{i,j\in W}\Re\!\left\{
 c_i\overline{c_j}K_{s_{ij}}(\omega_{ij})\right\},
 \quad
 C=\frac{4\rho g^2}{\pi U^2},
 \label{eq:pairresistance}
\end{equation}
where \(s_{ij}=n_i+n_j-2\geq4\),
\(\omega_{ij}=\nu(x_i-x_j)\), and
\begin{equation}
 K_s(\omega)=\int_1^\infty
 \frac{\e^{\iu\omega\lambda}}{
 \lambda^s\sqrt{\lambda^2-1}}\,d\lambda.
 \label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Submerged-pair error bound]
Let \(\mathcal P_S=\{(i,j):i\in S\ \text{or}\ j\in S\}\).  The resistance
discarded by replacing \(F\) with \(\widetilde F\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \left|\Rw-\widetilde R_{\mathrm w}\right|
 \leq C\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal P_S}
 |c_i c_j|\e^{-\nu(z_i+z_j)}K_{s_{ij}}(0).
 \label{eq:omitbound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We expand \(|F|^2-|\widetilde F|^2\) into precisely the ordered pairs in
\(\mathcal P_S\), apply the triangle inequality, use
\(|\e^{\iu\omega\lambda}|=1\), and bound
\(\e^{-\nu\lambda^2(z_i+z_j)}\leq\e^{-\nu(z_i+z_j)}\) for
\(\lambda\geq1\).  The remaining positive integral is \(K_{s_{ij}}(0)\).
\end{proof}

The zero-frequency kernel is analytic:
\begin{equation}
 K_s(0)=\int_0^\infty\cosh^{-s}t\,dt
 = \frac{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(s/2)}{2\Gamma((s+1)/2)},
 \label{eq:kzero}
\end{equation}
and we evaluate this kernel with a stable even/odd recurrence.

\subsection{Bickley kernels and contour quadrature}
\label{sec:contour}

Each retained endpoint pair is a familiar special function, and its contour
form removes the low-speed oscillation.  With \(\lambda=\cosh t\),
\cref{eq:kernel} becomes
\begin{equation}
 K_s(\omega)=\int_0^\infty
 \e^{\iu\omega\cosh t}\cosh^{-s}t\,dt
 =\Ki_s(-\iu\omega),
 \label{eq:bickley}
\end{equation}
where the final equality denotes analytic continuation of the Bickley
function described by \citet{BickleyNayler1935} and
\citet{DLMF1043}.\footnote{DLMF calls
\(\Ki_s\) the ``Bickley function'' and cites the 1935 paper by W.~G. Bickley
and J.~Nayler.  The compound spelling ``Bickley--Naylor'' occurs in later
literature, including \citet{RuffaToni2026}; it is not DLMF terminology.}
This identity supplies recurrences and other possible implementations.  The
present solver instead evaluates the contour directly.

The substitution \(\lambda=1+t^2\) removes the square-root endpoint in
\cref{eq:kernel}:
\begin{equation}
 K_s(\omega)=2\e^{\iu\omega}\int_0^\infty
 \frac{\e^{\iu\omega t^2}}{
 (1+t^2)^s\sqrt{2+t^2}}\,dt.
 \label{eq:tform}
\end{equation}
For \(\omega>0\), analyticity in the intervening sector permits the exact
rotation
\(t=\e^{\iu\pi/4}r/\sqrt\omega\), giving
\begin{equation}
 K_s(\omega)=\frac{2\e^{\iu(\omega+\pi/4)}}{\sqrt\omega}
 \int_0^\infty \frac{\e^{-r^2}\,dr}{
 (1+\iu r^2/\omega)^s\sqrt{2+\iu r^2/\omega}}.
 \label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
Conjugation gives the negative-frequency kernels.  The rotation replaces
oscillation with Gaussian decay and leaves only slowly varying frequency
dependence in the amplitude.  \citet{GibbsEtAl2020} obtains the same
frequency-independent principle in high-frequency scattering; here, the
quadratic phase gives the contour in closed form.

We take the principal square root in \cref{eq:tform}.  The
integrand's poles \(t=\pm\iu\) and branch points
\(t=\pm\iu\sqrt2\) lie outside the closed sector
\(0\leq\arg t\leq\pi/4\).  It is therefore analytic throughout the deformation.
On a closing arc \(t=R\e^{\iu\phi}\),
\(|\e^{\iu\omega t^2}|=\e^{-\omega R^2\sin(2\phi)}\leq1\), while the
algebraic factor including \(dt\) is \(O(R^{-2s})\).  The arc contribution
vanishes as \(R\to\infty\); thus the rotation is valid for every kernel order
used here, \(s\geq4\).

The implementation normally applies 24- and 48-point Gauss--Legendre rules on
\(0\leq r\leq8\).  When \(s/|\omega|\geq2\), the continued algebraic factor is
narrow near the origin, so the code uses 48- and 96-point rules.  The neglected
Gaussian tail obeys
\begin{equation}
 |K_s-K_s^{(8)}|\leq
 \frac{\e^{-64}}{8\sqrt{2\omega}},
 \qquad \omega>0,
 \label{eq:gausstail}
\end{equation}
using \(|1+\iu r^2/\omega|\geq1\),
\(|\sqrt{2+\iu r^2/\omega}|\geq\sqrt2\), and the standard Gaussian-tail
bound.  At the minimum supported nonzero frequency \(\omega=25\), this is
below \(4\times10^{-30}\).  The selected coarse/fine difference estimates
discretization error, but remains empirical.  The submerged-term and analytic
contour-tail bounds are rigorous; the total floating-point error is not
certified.  A committed fixed-rule scan over \(4\leq s\leq400\) and
\(|\omega|\in\{25,100,400\}\), using a 192-node same-contour reference and a
\(10^{-10}\) comparison floor, found a maximum relative 48-node discrepancy
of \(1.84\times10^{-11}\) through \(s=50\), but \(3.16\times10^{-1}\) above
that range.  The 24/48 difference covered every meaningful 48/192 discrepancy
by at least 2.22 in this scan.  This measured degree dependence motivates the
adaptive 48/96 rule within the range validated through \(s\leq98\); it is
evidence, not a quadrature theorem.

For fixed geometry, a nonzero endpoint pair costs either 72 or 144 scalar
nodes; increasing frequency never increases that cost.  Self pairs use
\cref{eq:kzero} without quadrature.  The biquadratic Wigley representation has
one longitudinal span and one vertical span.  Its 16 endpoint terms include
eight at the waterline.  Their full ordered-pair expansion contains 32
nonzero-frequency entries; conjugacy and upper-triangular accumulation reduce
these to 16 kernel evaluations.  Each uses the 24/48 rule, for
\(16\times72=1{,}152\) scalar nodes in all; equal-position pairs use
\cref{eq:kzero}.  A committed test checks each count.

\subsection{Selection rule and scope}
\label{sec:algorithm}

The endpoint reduction is useful only when the hull geometry and error estimate
support it.  The following rule selects either the reduced method or direct
adaptive integration of the outer integral:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We verify the physical conditions and exact spline geometry.
  \item We form and combine the exact endpoint terms in
        \cref{eq:endpointsum}.
  \item The method rejects the reduction unless the hull is upright and
        symmetric, its spline domain begins exactly at \(z=0\), and its
        longitudinal degree is at least one.
  \item It also rejects the reduction if any distinct retained endpoint pair
        has \(0<|\omega_{ij}|<25\).
  \item We evaluate \cref{eq:pairresistance}, the bound
        \cref{eq:omitbound}, and the weighted coarse/fine contour difference.
  \item The method accepts the reduced result if their combined relative
        estimate meets the requested tolerance; otherwise it reverts to direct
        real-axis integration.
\end{enumerate}

We give the acceptance calculation so that the final step can be reproduced.
If endpoint coefficient \(c_i\) combines \(a_i\) raw complex contributions
\(q_{ik}\) by compensated accumulation, we charge
\begin{equation}
 \delta_i=\gamma_{2a_i+2}\sum_k|q_{ik}|,\qquad
 \gamma_n=\frac{n\,u}{1-n\,u},
 \label{eq:coeffround}
\end{equation}
where \(u\) is binary64 machine epsilon.  For an upper-triangular retained
pair \((i,j)\), let \(m_{ij}=1\) on the diagonal and 2 otherwise, and let
\(K^{c}_{ij}\) and \(K^{f}_{ij}\) be the coarse and fine contour values.  The
three absolute resistance-level components are
\begin{align}
 B_o &= C\!\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal P_S}
 |c_i c_j|\e^{-\nu(z_i+z_j)}K_{s_{ij}}(0),
 \label{eq:bo}\\
 B_q &= C\!\sum_{i\leq j}m_{ij}|c_i c_j|
 |K^{f}_{ij}-K^{c}_{ij}|,
 \label{eq:bq}\\
 B_r &= C\!\sum_{i,j}
 (\delta_i|c_j|+|c_i|\delta_j+\delta_i\delta_j)
 \e^{-\nu(z_i+z_j)}K_{s_{ij}}(0).
 \label{eq:br}
\end{align}
Here \(B_o\) is the omission bound in \cref{eq:omitbound}, \(B_q\) is the
weighted contour-difference estimate, and \(B_r\) accounts for cancellation
while constructing the endpoint map.  A separate coefficient-construction
floor is
\begin{equation}
 \epsilon_c=\max(2\times10^{-8},\,4r_{\rm span}),
 \label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
where \(r_{\rm span}\) is the independently sampled relative residual when
the local power basis reconstructs \(f_x\).  Set \(B=B_o+B_q+B_r\).  If
\(B\geq|\widetilde R_{\rm w}|\), or any quantity is nonfinite, the method
rejects the reduced result.  Otherwise the reported estimate used for
selection is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\epsilon=\epsilon_c+
 \frac{B}{|\widetilde R_{\rm w}|-B}.
 \label{eq:dispatcherror}
\end{equation}
The denominator follows from the reverse triangle inequality; using
\(B/|\widetilde R_{\rm w}|\) would understate an error relative to the unknown
exact resistance.  \(B_o\), the separate Gaussian-tail inequality, and
\(B_r\) under the standard floating-point model are analytic bounds.
\(B_q\) and the calibrated floor \(\epsilon_c\) are empirical estimates.
Consequently \(\widehat\epsilon\) is a selection diagnostic, not a formal
interval certificate.

The frequency condition reserves the fixed contour for sufficiently rapid
oscillation.  For the Wigley bow--stern pair,
\(\omega=\nu L=gL/U^2=1/\Fn^2\), which equals the implementation's
\(\nu|x_i-x_j|\).  Thus \(\omega\geq25\) corresponds to \(\Fn\leq0.2\).  The
same condition quantifies the geometry restriction.  For \(m\) equal
longitudinal spans whose adjacent endpoints remain active,
\(\omega_{\rm adj}=1/(m\Fn^2)\), so the spacing condition requires
\begin{equation}
 m\leq\frac{1}{25\Fn^2}.
 \label{eq:spangate}
\end{equation}
At \(\Fn=0.05\), for example, this permits at most 16 such spans.  A retained
multi-span endpoint map with 360 distinct nonzero-frequency pairs would use
\(360\times72=25{,}920\) ordinary-rule nodes, compared with 1,152 for the
16-pair Wigley map; this is node-count arithmetic, not a runtime benchmark.
One closer endpoint invalidates the specialized path for the whole hull.  The
selection rule then bases the final choice on the actual hull, speed, and
resistance cancellation rather than on nominal Froude number alone.  At the
default relative tolerance \(10^{-5}\), it rejects the Wigley reduction at
\(\Fn=0.08\) and accepts it at \(\Fn=0.05\).

The implementation uses safe Rust and no external numerical library.  Tests
check the reduced solver at three independent levels: exact inner
decomposition, isolated Bickley kernels, and total resistance.  The broader
validation suite checks Froude similarity, zero camber for symmetric sides,
classical catamaran \(4\cos^2\) interference, tolerance self-convergence, and
reported against observed error.

\section{Numerical results}
\label{sec:results}

We test the reduction against independently constructed endpoint-aware
references, a published resistance value, and direct outer integration.

\subsection{Independent reference}

The standard Wigley hull provides an analytic inner transform and a demanding
low-speed outer integral.  Its half-breadth is
\begin{equation}
 f(x,z)=\frac{B}{2}\left(1-\frac{4x^2}{L^2}\right)
 \left(1-\frac{z^2}{T^2}\right),
 \quad |x|\leq L/2,\quad 0\leq z\leq T,
 \label{eq:wigley}
\end{equation}
with \(L=\SI{10}{m}\), \(B=\SI{1}{m}\), and
\(T=\SI{0.625}{m}\).  We constructed the primary reference independently of
the production moment code and outer integrator.  It evaluates the analytic
Wigley transform on the positive real axis after \(\lambda=1+t^2\), which
converts the outer weight to
\(2\lambda^2|F(\lambda)|^2/\sqrt{2+t^2}\).  At \(\lambda=1\),
\citet[p.~371]{Tuck1989} identifies the square-root singularity and recommends
removing it before quadrature; this transformation does so.

We first construct panels in \(\lambda\):
\(\Delta\lambda=\pi/(\nu L)\), so either endpoint phase
\(\nu\lambda L/2\) advances by at most \(\pi/2\).  We then map the endpoints

\subsection{Runtime}

We timed \texttt{cargo bench -p michell --bench wigley} with 30 samples per
case, the optimized Cargo bench profile, no custom \texttt{RUSTFLAGS}, and the
default requested relative tolerance \(10^{-5}\).  Each case received an
unmeasured warm call.  We timed calls below \SI{0.1}{ms} in batches of 256 and
divided by 256; separate untimed calls produced the checksums, so timer and
checksum work do not overlap.  The two method comparisons alternated execution
order on every sample.  We sampled the other cases separately in printed
order.  The host was a 10-core Apple M5 MacBook Air with 24 GB RAM, arm64
Darwin 25.5.0, Rust/Cargo 1.96.0, and LLVM 22.1.2.  Absolute timings depend on
the host; work counts and checksums are deterministic.

Against that lineage, we make five engineering changes.  We operate span by
span on tensor-product B-splines, including full-multiplicity chine knots, and
restrict both spline degrees to the independently validated range 1--16 while
charging coefficient construction and endpoint cancellation.  We bound every
omitted submerged endpoint pair, evaluate retained \(K_s\) pairs on a
frequency-scaled contour with a bounded, order-aware node count, and accept the
reduction only when the combined estimate meets the requested tolerance.
Otherwise, the method returns to direct integration.

These changes improve the implementation but make no claim of priority.  A full
method-by-method benchmark against the Michelsen and Sendagorta--Grases
representations lies outside our scope and remains future work.

The endpoint structure has an independent history.
\citet{KellerAhluwalia1976} express leading small-Froude resistance and waves
through bow and stern waterline slopes.  \citet[pp.~83--85]{Gotman2002}
derives finite endpoint-derivative sums, forms their products in resistance,
and separates bow, stern, and bow--stern contributions.  Gotman's construction
is closer to the present pair structure than a generic integration-by-parts
citation.  We put that mechanism into finite B-spline form without introducing
a new physical asymptotic principle.

Tuck and Lazauskas also establish exact or Filon-like integration of
piecewise-polynomial inner data.
\citet[sec.~6.5, pp.~6-10--6-12]{Lazauskas2009} gives exact
piecewise-quadratic hull integrals, compares equally spaced Simpson and
cosine-spaced trapezoidal angular rules, and reports that very low Froude
number remains exceptional.  The accessible thesis supports those numerical
practices but does not, by itself, document the internals of every Michlet
release.  For accepted B-spline hulls, our solver retains the same thin-ship
physics while keeping low-Froude outer work from growing with frequency.  This
is an implementation improvement, not a head-to-head performance claim against
the Michlet executable.

Exact endpoint reduction also survives the outer modulus square: pair
expansion converts it into a small set of reusable special-function kernels.
\citet{RuffaToni2026} gives finite Bessel--Struve module representations for
integer-order Bickley functions.  Their stability on the imaginary axis
remains to be tested as a possible kernel backend.

\citet{HuybrechsVandewalle2006} establishes contour deformation for
oscillatory quadrature.  \citet{Motygin2017} applies steepest descent and
Clenshaw--Curtis quadrature in ship-wave theory, but to the oscillatory part of
the Kelvin Green function rather than Michell's resistance integral.
\citet{GibbsHewettHuybrechs2024} automates more general phases and coalescing
saddles.  Such methods could treat multihull transverse phases; the present
quadratic phase needs only the closed contour in \cref{eq:gaussian}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:limits}

Several limitations should be borne in mind.  The evidence covers upright
symmetric monohulls in linear, deep-water thin-ship theory.  Within that model,
the reduction removes a numerical obstacle; it does not extend the physical
theory.

The geometry range is narrow.  The reduced solver supports upright symmetric
single hulls of degree 1--16 in each spline direction.  Active endpoints must
satisfy \(\nu|x_i-x_j|\geq25\), so the method applies to coarse,
well-separated endpoint maps.  Asymmetric, heeled, multihull, and closely
spaced cases revert to direct integration.

The contour rule also has a measured degree limit.  The fixed 48-node rule was
accurate through \(s\simeq50\) and degraded beyond it in scans to \(s=400\);
its 24/48 difference nevertheless remained conservative against the 192-node
same-contour comparator throughout that scan.  The implementation therefore
uses 48/96 when \(s/|\omega|\geq2\).  The range over which the contour method
has been validated ends at the independently tested maximum \(s=98\).

The error components do not all have the same status.  The submerged-pair
omission bound and Gaussian tail bound are analytic.  The selected coarse/fine
quadrature difference and floating-point roundoff are not interval-certified.

The independent references have their own resolution floor.  The \(1+t^2\)
and \(\sec\theta\) calculations use compensated summation through
\(\lambda=4000\).  At \(\Fn=0.02\), their cutoff-doubling changes are
\(1.38\times10^{-15}\) and \(1.23\times10^{-15}\), respectively.
Differences near the observed \(10^{-13}\) scale measure resolution, not
interval-certified digits.

Michell theory remains linear, inviscid, slender, deep-water, and
fixed-attitude.  At very low speed, viscous effects may dominate the already
small wave resistance.  \citet{TrinhChapman2015} shows that low-Froude waves
past singular free-surface obstacles can be exponentially small, beyond all
algebraic orders, and controlled by Stokes phenomena.  Better quadrature does
not resolve that different problem.

Multihull support offers the clearest numerical extension.  A transverse
placement introduces the phase \(\nu y\lambda\sqrt{\lambda^2-1}\) and moving
saddles absent from \cref{eq:gaussian}; a uniform or automated contour must
account for them.  Retaining shallow submerged endpoints likewise produces
mixed linear and quadratic complex phases.  Further work should certify the
contour error, test imaginary-argument Bickley recurrences, derive finite-depth
kernels, and differentiate the endpoint representation for low-Froude hull
optimization.

\section{Conclusions}

Finite integration by parts converts the B-spline inner transform into endpoint
waves.  Pair expansion then turns the outer modulus square into continued
Bickley kernels, and contour rotation replaces real-axis oscillation with
Gaussian decay.  Geometry, rather than Froude number, sets the reduced
quadrature work.  The submerged-pair bound lets the selection rule reject the
reduction whenever its estimated error exceeds the requested tolerance.

For the standard Wigley hull at \(\Fn=0.02\), the reduced solver is more
accurate than direct outer integration and about 700 times faster in the
frozen paired run.  This advantage is confined to the supported geometry and
rests on the analytic reduction lineage summarized in \cref{tab:lineage}.
Claims beyond the current scope require broader geometry and certified total
error control.
